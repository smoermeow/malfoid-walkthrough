# Building an Anime Short With ChatGPT Through Iteration

This is not actually my normal scripting workflow.

Normally, I use an agent with dedicated skills and knowledge that already guide it toward some of the things I want visually and structurally.

For these videos, I did something much less organized.

I basically winged the script development inside a normal ChatGPT conversation.

Episode 1 ended up working surprisingly well, and looking back at the conversation, I think there are parts of that process that could be useful to other people.

Not because there was one great prompt.

There wasn't.

The useful part was the iteration.

I brought the story, characters, visual ideas and preferences. ChatGPT helped structure and develop that material. I kept reacting to what it produced, changing direction when something felt wrong and gradually making the script more specific.

This is a walkthrough of that process.

---

## TL;DR — for the lazy ones

I gave ChatGPT the story and rough visual direction, then developed the script through iteration rather than one giant prompt.

The biggest change came when the first camera sequence felt too conventional. We flipped it from:

**full geography → closer shots**

to:

**close fragments → gradual reveal → full geography**

From there I kept pushing toward the visual language I wanted: **foreshortening, stronger anime perspectives, low/high angles, canted shots, POVs, ground-level views and less generic shot/reverse-shot coverage.**

I reused my normal shot format:

**Duration / Camera / Action / Dialogue / SFX / Lighting / VFX**

and we added:

**Global visual style / Voice anchors / Global lighting / Plot summary**

The useful part was not one perfect prompt. It was reacting when something felt off and continuing to direct the AI until the sequence matched the vibe I wanted.

---

# Full Walkthrough

## 1. I started with the Episode 1 idea

I already had the actual premise and characters.

I brought that material into the conversation and used ChatGPT to help turn it into a scene.

The important distinction here is that ChatGPT was not being asked to invent the project for me.

I was giving it the material and then using the conversation to develop it.

There was also no carefully designed workflow at this point.

I was just working through the idea in the chat.

---

## 2. We started turning the story into a visual sequence

Once we had the scene to work with, we started figuring out how it should actually be shown.

The first direction was fairly conventional.

We started by establishing the larger geography and then moving progressively closer.

Basically:

**full geography → closer shots**

There was nothing technically broken about that.

It just did not feel like what I wanted.

That is important, because I was not following some predefined rule that said the sequence had to be structured differently.

The vibe was off.

So we talked about it and tried another direction.

---

## 3. We reversed the opening

Instead of establishing everything first, we turned the sequence around.

We began much closer and revealed the situation piece by piece.

The resulting opening moved through things like:

**Voldemort's feet**

→ **Harry on the ground**

→ **Malfoid and the surrounding Death Eaters**

→ **Voldemort**

→ **the wider overhead geography**

The finished Episode 1 script still shows that progression: a low-angle close shot of Voldemort's boots, a tight shot of Harry, a shot across Malfoid and the watching ring, another closer Voldemort/Harry exchange, and then the canted overhead wide revealing the complete situation.

That change gave the opening a very different feeling.

Instead of explaining everything immediately, the viewer gets fragments and gradually understands the situation.

But this was not something I had mapped out as a formal technique beforehand.

The original sequence did not feel right, we discussed another direction, changed it, and the new version worked better.

---

## 4. I kept feeding more of the visual feel into the conversation

This was a normal ChatGPT conversation, not one of my dedicated skills.

That meant the model did not start with a knowledge base already telling it what I mean when I want a certain visual style.

So as we worked, I kept adding more of that direction myself.

Things like:

- foreshortening
- stronger anime perspectives
- unusual camera placement
- low angles
- high angles
- canted perspectives
- POV
- ground-level shots
- more aggressive three-quarter views
- less generic coverage

The finished Episode 1 sequence ended up with a pretty wide range of perspectives: low-angle close shots, tight three-quarter shots, a canted overhead wide, rear three-quarter views, Harry's grounded OTS and POV angles, ground-level framing, high-angle reveals, extreme close-ups and lateral compositions.

That diversity was not all present from the first attempt.

The visual language accumulated while I kept directing the conversation toward the kind of feel I wanted.

---

## 5. In a way, the conversation was building the missing skill context

This is one of the more interesting things looking back at it.

In my normal setup, I have skills where some of this kind of information can already exist in the knowledge base.

Here it did not.

So the more I talked about things like foreshortening, perspective and the feel I wanted, the more creative context the conversation had to work with.

I was effectively supplying that guidance as we went.

That may be part of why Episode 1 improved through the iteration.

I am not saying that this is technically the same thing as building a skill.

It isn't.

But from my side of the conversation, I was gradually giving ChatGPT more information about what I wanted it to aim for.

---

## 6. I was directing the sequence by feel as much as by technical decisions

Some corrections were specific.

Other times I did not have a precise technical diagnosis.

Something just felt wrong.

That was still enough to continue working.

Instead of needing to know:

> The problem is specifically the camera elevation in Shot 4.

I could say that the sequence was not doing what I hoped it would do, we could discuss another direction, and then change it.

The opening is the clearest example of that.

I did not start with a rule saying:

> Never establish the geography first.

We tried it.

I did not like the result.

We changed it.

That is much closer to how the actual process worked than pretending I was applying a fixed filmmaking framework from the beginning.

---

## 7. I used the same shot format I already use in my prompts

Once we were building the actual shot script, I reused a format I already use for generation prompts.

That format was:

```text
## SHOT XX

**Duration:**

**Camera:**

**Action:**

**Dialogue:**

**SFX:**

**Lighting:**

**VFX:**
```

This was not a format ChatGPT invented for me.

I already worked with those fields and copied the structure into the script.

### Duration

How long the shot should run.

### Camera

The actual shot setup and perspective.

### Action

What happens physically inside the shot.

### Dialogue

The dialogue happening during the shot.

### SFX

The sound information.

### Lighting

The lighting for that particular shot.

### VFX

Any visual effects needed for the shot.

The useful thing about this was that the script we were developing was already structurally close to the way I would later prompt individual generations.

---

## 8. We added a global layer around that shot format

As we worked, we also ended up with information that sat above the individual shots:

```text
# Global visual style

# Voice anchors

# Global lighting

# Plot summary
```

These gave us somewhere to keep things that needed to remain consistent across the sequence instead of rebuilding them inside every shot.

### Global visual style

The common visual direction.

### Voice anchors

A consistent voice description for each recurring character.

### Global lighting

The shared lighting conditions for the scene.

### Plot summary

The overall situation the individual shots belong to.

So there were effectively two levels:

**global episode context**

and

**individual shot instructions**

The shot format came from the way I already prompted.

The global layer developed around it while we were building these episodes.

## 9. Episode 1 then became the reference for Episode 2

When we moved on to Episode 2, we first went back to what we had made in Episode 1.

That mattered because Episode 1 now contained examples of the camera language and shot variety we had developed.

The finished Episode 1 script had things like:

- low-angle close shots
- tight three-quarter framing
- eye-level shots where they actually made sense
- canted overhead wides
- rear three-quarter shots
- grounded Harry POVs
- over-the-shoulder compositions
- high-angle reveals
- low lateral frames
- extreme low-angle POVs
- extreme close-ups
- long lateral shots

So we had something concrete to refer back to rather than just saying:

> Make Episode 2 cinematic.

---

## 10. Episode 2 also needed a different kind of storytelling

Episode 2 was much more dialogue-driven.

One of the central ideas was that Harry is ranting while Malfoid is thinking over him.

Harry does not conveniently stop speaking every time we hear her thoughts.

His argument continues.

Malfoid becomes increasingly distracted by him, and her inner dialogue starts taking focus while Harry's voice becomes less important to her.

That also meant the SFX field had to do more than list environmental sounds.

It needed to describe the changing audio perspective:

- Harry speaking normally
- Malfoid's thoughts entering
- Harry receding in the mix
- her internal voice taking focus
- Harry returning to full presence when she snaps back to reality

So the same shot structure could also carry more complicated sound direction.

---

## 11. Episode 2 did not go nearly as well

Episode 1 came together surprisingly well through the improvised back-and-forth.

Episode 2 became much more of a struggle.

I do not know exactly why.

By that point the conversation had accumulated a lot of information and revisions, so context may have played a part, but that is only a possibility.

What actually happened is easier to describe.

We started losing things that had already been established.

For example:

- Harry's rant was supposed to continue underneath Malfoid's thoughts, but the dialogue got broken into separate shots.
- At one point the script said that Harry “continues” speaking without actually writing what he was saying.
- The audio treatment did not initially explain properly what should happen to Harry's voice when Malfoid's inner dialogue becomes the focus.
- The camera work became much more static than the Episode 1 reference.
- A long buildup toward Foideldore was supposed to work over Harry and Malfoid continuing to argue, but the dialogue that gave the shot its purpose was no longer actually present in the shot.
- Fixing individual problems sometimes introduced new problems elsewhere.

That made the iteration much less productive than it had been during Episode 1.

---

## 12. That also highlighted the difference between this and using a dedicated skill

With one of my normal skills, more of the visual and structural direction can already exist before the conversation begins.

Here I had to keep establishing that direction manually.

That worked surprisingly well for Episode 1.

It also meant that a lot of important information eventually lived inside one very long conversation.

Whether that is specifically why Episode 2 became harder, I do not know.

But it is one clear difference between this experiment and my normal workflow.

# What I take from it

This was not a carefully planned prompting system.

I did not start with a perfect template for directing ChatGPT.

I had an idea, started developing it in the chat, reacted to what I got back and kept pushing it toward the visual language I wanted.

Sometimes that meant very concrete direction.

Sometimes it was simply:

**this doesn't feel right.**

Then we explored another version.

The biggest example was the opening:

**full geography → closer shots**

became:

**close fragments → gradual reveal → full geography**

At the same time I kept feeding more of the visual language I wanted into the conversation — especially things like foreshortening and stronger anime perspectives.

The shot format I already used for generation gave us a practical structure to put all of that into.

And over time, the conversation itself accumulated enough context that Episode 1 developed a pretty distinct visual language.

That is the part of the experiment I think is useful.

Not a magic prompt.

Not a formal workflow I secretly followed.

Just **giving the AI material, reacting to what it gives back, adding more of your own taste and direction, and iterating until the sequence starts doing what you wanted it to do.**
